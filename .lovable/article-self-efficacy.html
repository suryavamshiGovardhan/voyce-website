Let's nerd out properly. Not just definitions. We're going to understand how a human being develops belief in their own abilities, how repeated criticism can damage that belief, why some people eventually stop trying, and how they can rebuild their sense of agency.

There is a fascinating paradox at the centre of this subject:

A person can possess the ability to do something but still believe they are incapable of doing it.

And the reverse is also possible: someone can believe they are capable of something without yet possessing the necessary skills.

That gap between actual capability and perceived capability is where much of this psychology lives.

PART 1 — SELF-EFFICACY

1. What exactly is self-efficacy?

The concept was developed by Canadian-American psychologist Albert Bandura, particularly through his 1977 paper, Self-Efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change.

His central proposition was that human behaviour is influenced not only by what people know or can do, but also by what they believe they can do.

Self-efficacy is an individual's belief in their capacity to organise and execute the actions required to achieve a particular result.

Let's break that definition down.

  • Self = yourself.
  • Efficacy = the capacity to produce an intended effect.
  • Self-efficacy = your belief that you can successfully perform the actions needed for a task.

Imagine two students.

Student A

  • Knows mathematics.
  • Has solved similar problems before.
  • Believes they can solve a difficult problem.
  • Approaches the problem despite uncertainty.

Student B

  • Has similar mathematical knowledge.
  • Has similar previous experience.
  • Believes they are bad at mathematics.
  • Avoids attempting the problem.

Their actual capabilities might be almost identical.

But their beliefs about their capabilities are different.

That's self-efficacy.

An important distinction

ConceptWhat it means
Self-esteemHow positively you evaluate yourself
Self-confidenceA broad, everyday sense of confidence
Self-efficacyBelief in your ability to execute a particular task
Self-conceptYour overall understanding of who you are
Self-worthYour perceived value as a person

You can have high self-esteem but low mathematical self-efficacy.

You can also have low self-esteem but high professional self-efficacy.

Self-efficacy is task-specific. It is not a measurement of your worth as a human being.

2. Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy

This is one of the most important parts of the entire theory.

Bandura proposed four major sources through which people develop beliefs about their capabilities. The American Psychological Association (APA) explains these in its research material on self-efficacy.

1. Mastery experiences (most influential source)

Your own experience of succeeding or failing at something.

Example: You build your first Arduino circuit. It doesn't work. You troubleshoot it, identify the wiring error and finally make the LED blink.

Your brain now has evidence: I can solve technical problems.

2. Vicarious experiences

Learning through observing other people.

Example: You see someone with a similar background build a robotics project.

You begin thinking: If someone like me can do it, perhaps I can do it too.

3. Verbal persuasion

How encouragement, feedback and other people's words influence your belief in your ability.

Example: A teacher tells you, "Your approach is correct. You need to improve your debugging process."

This can help you believe that improvement is possible.

4. Physiological and emotional states

How you interpret your bodily and emotional experiences.

Example: Before a presentation, your heart beats faster.

  • Interpretation A: "I'm nervous. I'm going to fail."
  • Interpretation B: "My body is activated because this matters to me."

The same physical sensation can influence your perceived capability differently.

Here's the interesting part

Suppose you want to become a good robotics instructor.

Your self-efficacy doesn't magically appear because you repeat: "I'm a great robotics instructor."

It develops through a combination of:

  • Successfully teaching a small robotics lesson.
  • Watching other instructors teach.
  • Receiving useful feedback.
  • Learning to manage the anxiety of teaching.

This is why experience is often more powerful than motivational language.

A belief becomes stronger when reality repeatedly provides evidence for it.

PART 2 — LEARNED HELPLESSNESS

3. What is learned helplessness?

The concept is historically associated with psychologists Martin E. P. Seligman and Steven F. Maier.

Their foundational experimental research began in the 1960s.

Learned helplessness describes a pattern in which repeated exposure to situations perceived as uncontrollable can undermine the expectation that one's actions will change outcomes.

In simpler language:

A person repeatedly experiences situations in which their actions seem ineffective.

Eventually, they may stop attempting to change those situations, even when opportunities to do so become available.

The famous experiment

In 1967, Seligman and Maier published research involving dogs exposed to uncontrollable aversive stimulation.

The historical experimental design involved three groups:

  • Group A could terminate the unpleasant stimulation through an available response.
  • Group B received stimulation that was not controllable by their own responses.
  • Group C served as a comparison group.

In a later escape-avoidance task, the researchers observed that some animals previously exposed to uncontrollable stimulation did not attempt to escape, even when escape was possible.

This helped establish the original learned helplessness theory.

However, there's a very important scientific update.

In their 2016 review, Learned Helplessness at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience, Maier and Seligman revisited the original theory. They explained that the biological mechanisms were more complicated than initially understood: passivity during prolonged aversive experiences could be a default defensive response, while learning that control is possible helps animals overcome it.

So don't reduce the entire phenomenon to "people learn to be weak." That's scientifically inaccurate.

4. How learned helplessness can develop in everyday human life

Consider a hypothetical student.

Stage 1 — Initial failure

The student attempts a mathematics examination and fails.

Stage 2 — Repeated negative experiences

The student studies, but receives poor marks. Their teacher criticises them. Their family compares them with other students.

Stage 3 — Formation of expectations

"No matter what I do, I will fail."

Stage 4 — Withdrawal

The student stops asking questions, avoids difficult problems and reduces effort.

Stage 5 — Reinforcement

Because the student practises less, their performance may deteriorate, reinforcing the original belief.

Self-reinforcing cycle.

Notice something.

The original problem was poor performance.

But the problem eventually becomes the person's expectation about the future.

They are no longer responding only to the difficulty of mathematics. They are responding to what they believe mathematics will always mean for them.

This is an important distinction between experiencing failure and developing helplessness.

Failure is an event. Helplessness is a possible pattern of learning and expectation.

5. Three psychological dimensions worth understanding

Learned helplessness is often discussed through three explanatory dimensions in the reformulated theory of attribution.

DimensionQuestionExample
Internal vs externalWhere do I locate the cause?"I'm incapable" vs "The teaching method was poor."
Stable vs unstableWill it remain the same?"I'll always fail" vs "I struggled this time."
Global vs specificDoes it affect everything?"I'm bad at everything" vs "I'm struggling with algebra."

These are related to explanatory style, not a diagnostic test for learned helplessness.

Consider two students who fail the same examination.

Student A: "I failed because I didn't understand this particular chapter. I'll change my study method."

Student B: "I failed because I'm stupid. I'll never understand anything."

Same event. Different interpretation.

Different expectations about what happens next.

And potentially different behaviour.

PART 3 — THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE TWO

6. Self-efficacy vs learned helplessness

These concepts are related, but they are not exact opposites.

Self-efficacyLearned helplessness
Belief that I can perform an actionExpectation that my actions won't change outcomes
Encourages task engagementCan encourage withdrawal
Supports persistenceCan undermine persistence
Can grow through masteryCan emerge after uncontrollable experiences
"I can improve this.""Nothing I do matters."

Think of them as two different ways of interpreting your relationship with the world.

A simplified psychological model

Agency — "My actions can make a difference."

Experiment → Feedback → Learning → Improved capability

Helplessness — "My actions won't make a difference."

Uncontrollability → Reduced expectation of control → Withdrawal

Conceptual illustration, not a clinical diagnostic model.

Here is the subtle distinction:

You can have low self-efficacy without learned helplessness.

For example, you might say: "I don't know how to operate this machine yet."

That is not helplessness. It may simply be an accurate assessment of your present skill.

But if you say: "There's no point learning this machine. Nothing I do will ever work."

Now you're expressing an expectation of uncontrollability.

That expectation is much closer to the territory of learned helplessness.

PART 4 — HOW CRITICISM AFFECTS SELF-EFFICACY

7. The psychology of criticism

This is where things get particularly interesting.

Criticism is not inherently harmful.

In fact, constructive criticism is essential for learning. The psychological impact depends on its content, delivery, frequency, source, context and the meaning the receiver assigns to it.

Let's distinguish two kinds.

Constructive criticism

"Your explanation was difficult to follow. Try breaking the concept into smaller steps."

Psychological interpretation:

  • The problem is identifiable.
  • The behaviour can be changed.
  • There is a path to improvement.
  • The person's identity remains separate from the mistake.

Likely message: I have something to improve.

Identity-based criticism

"You're useless. You never understand anything."

Psychological interpretation:

  • The criticism attacks the person's identity.
  • It offers no actionable solution.
  • It generalises one performance into an overall judgement.
  • It can create shame and fear of future failure.

Possible message: Something is fundamentally wrong with me.

The criticism → self-efficacy pathway

  • Repeated negative criticism — especially when it is personal, humiliating or uncontrollable
  • → Negative self-appraisal — "Maybe I'm not capable."
  • → Reduced task-specific self-efficacy — "I don't think I can do this."
  • → Avoidance and reduced attempts — less practice, less experimentation, fewer opportunities to succeed
  • → Possible reinforcement of helplessness — "See? I really can't do anything."

This is a possible psychological feedback loop, not an inevitable consequence of criticism.

Five mechanisms through which criticism can affect you

1. Internalisation

You begin accepting someone else's judgement as your own.

Someone says: "You're not intelligent." Initially, you might disagree.

But if you repeatedly hear the same judgement from people whose opinions matter to you, you may eventually start using it to evaluate yourself.

This is called internalisation: incorporating external beliefs or standards into your own psychological framework.

2. Social comparison

You evaluate your abilities by comparing yourself with others.

  • Your colleague delivers a presentation.
  • Your manager praises them.
  • You receive criticism.
  • You conclude that the colleague is naturally more capable.

But you may be comparing their polished performance with your unfinished learning process.

Social comparison can distort your perception of your actual abilities.

3. Threat appraisal

Criticism can make a task feel psychologically threatening.

Instead of thinking: "I'm going to learn something." You begin thinking: "What if I make a mistake and everybody judges me?"

The task now carries two burdens:

  • Completing the task.
  • Protecting yourself from embarrassment.

This can consume attention and interfere with performance.

4. Self-fulfilling expectations

You expect failure, so you avoid the task or invest less effort.

Your performance may then deteriorate, seemingly confirming your original expectation.

The expectation contributes to the outcome it predicted.

5. Attributional bias

You interpret failure as evidence of a permanent personal deficiency.

  • "I failed because my preparation was insufficient."
  • "I failed because I'm fundamentally incapable."

The second explanation is broader and more damaging because it treats a specific outcome as evidence about your entire identity.

Research on explanatory styles and helplessness explores how these interpretations relate to motivation and depressive thinking.

8. Can criticism actually destroy self-efficacy?

Let's be precise.

Criticism does not automatically destroy self-efficacy. Nor does receiving negative feedback mean you have learned helplessness.

The impact depends on several variables:

VariablePotential psychological effect
FrequencyRepeated negative evaluation can accumulate
AuthorityFeedback from trusted or powerful people may carry greater weight
SpecificitySpecific feedback is easier to use
ControllabilityChangeable problems can preserve agency
Previous experienceStrong mastery experiences may buffer a setback
Social environmentSupport can help restore confidence

Bandura's theory helps explain why verbal persuasion matters, but also why direct mastery experiences are more dependable evidence of personal capability.

Here's a question worth thinking about:

What happens when the person criticising you becomes the person whose approval you need to believe you're capable?

You might gradually stop evaluating your own performance independently. You start asking:

  • Did they approve of me?
  • Did they appreciate my work?
  • Did they think I was intelligent?
  • Did I disappoint them?

Your sense of capability can become excessively dependent on external evaluation.

This is not a formal diagnosis. It is a useful way to understand how social approval and self-evaluation can become entangled.

PART 5 — THE PHILOSOPHICAL DIMENSION

Psychology explains the mechanisms. Philosophy asks a different question:

What does it mean to be an agent who can act, choose, fail and change?

9. Aristotle — Potentiality and actuality

Aristotle distinguished between:

  • Dynamis: potentiality or capacity.
  • Energeia: actuality or being at work.

Imagine a person who has the potential to become a skilled robotics instructor.

That potential alone doesn't make them skilled. They must exercise their abilities.

They must teach, make mistakes, practise, observe, adapt and repeat.

This creates a useful philosophical connection to self-efficacy.

Your potential is not the same as your demonstrated capability.

And your current performance is not necessarily the limit of your potential.

Philosophical takeaway: You discover and develop capacities through their exercise.

A person who never attempts something cannot acquire the same experiential knowledge as someone who repeatedly engages with it.
